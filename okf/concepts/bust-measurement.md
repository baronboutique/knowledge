# Bust Measurement

The question we are asked more than any other in women's measuring is not how tight to pull the tape. It is where the bust actually is. Someone standing alone with a tape wants to know which part of the body we mean before she worries about technique, and that is a reasonable place to start.

## Where the tape goes

The tape passes around the fullest part of the bust at the front and stays level with that point around the back. It should not slope down at the back or ride up. It should form a level line around the body, with the customer standing naturally while the tape is positioned.

If the fullest point is hard to identify in a mirror, place the tape around the bust and check the position from the side. The tape should cross the area where the bust projects furthest from the body. What matters is that the tape is measuring the full circumference of the bust, not the upper chest above it and not the rib cage below it. Those are the two directions this measurement drifts, and both are common.

This is also why we do not accept a bust measurement estimated from bra size. The tape has to describe the actual body.

## Bust, chest, and bra size are three different things

Our women's sets ask for both bra size and bust circumference, and customers sometimes assume one is redundant. They are not.

Bra size tells us about the relationship between the bust and the rib cage, and about how the customer is habitually fitted. Bust circumference tells us the actual measurement around the fullest part. We want both because together they describe proportions that neither describes alone.

Two women with the same bust circumference can wear different bra sizes, because their rib cages and bust shapes differ. Two women in the same bra size can have quite different overall proportions. For a garment drafted from scratch, that distinction is the whole point. We are not building a garment that corresponds to a bra size. We are building one around the customer's actual proportions.

## Why we usually do not ask for the under-bust

Standard size charts often request an under-bust or rib measurement. We do not routinely require it, and the reason is practical rather than dismissive: it is not necessary for every pattern we draft, and every additional measurement makes self-measuring more complicated and more error-prone for the customer.

We ask for the measurements relevant to the particular garment and work out proportions from what we already have. Where the bust-to-rib-cage relationship is unusual, or the design makes that area structurally important, the under-bust becomes useful and we ask for it directly. We do not collect measurements simply because they appear on a chart.

## What goes wrong, and what it does to the garment

The most common faults are taking the measurement too high or too low, measuring over clothing, and wearing a bra that changes the natural position of the bust.

A wrong bust measurement does not simply make a garment slightly too big or too small. It changes how the entire upper body of the garment sits. Too small, and the garment pulls across the bust, creates tension running toward the armhole, causes the front to ride up, or makes the closure pull apart. Too large, and the bust area hangs loose while the rest of the garment fits correctly, which reads as a badly made garment even though every other measurement was right.

We catch suspicious numbers before cutting by reading the relationships. If the bust does not make sense beside the bra size, shoulder measurement, waist, hips, height, or photographs, we investigate rather than accept it. The test garment is the second safeguard: when the numbers alone cannot settle the question, the garment on the body does.

## Bodies the chart does not fit

A size chart is a reference, not a person. A fuller bust with a smaller rib cage, a very small bust, noticeable asymmetry, a post-surgery body, pregnancy, or a customer between conventional bra sizes will all produce measurements that sit awkwardly on a standard chart.

We do not force those customers into the chart. We work from the actual measurements, the garment design, and what the customer tells us about how she wants it to fit.

If something about the bust could affect the pattern, it belongs in the notes: a significant difference between left and right, a change in bust position, post-surgical changes, pregnancy, or anything else that means the single circumference does not tell the whole story. Photographs help here too, because they show proportions that numbers cannot always explain. The more unusual the body or the fit requirement, the more valuable accurate information becomes.

No customer should feel her body is the problem. Understanding the body and drafting accordingly is the tailor's job, not the customer's.

## What one silk jacket taught us

A customer ordering a Dupioni silk jacket took her measurements over a T-shirt, without the bra she normally wears with that kind of garment. The issue was not only the added thickness of the shirt. The absent bra changed the natural position of the bustline so that the bust sat lower during measuring, and the resulting chest circumference came out smaller than it should have been.

We did not try to correct the number by guessing what it ought to have been. The test garment showed us the problem. Once we had photographs and her feedback, the cause was clear. We asked her to remeasure correctly, then sent a second test garment to verify the revised numbers before going near the silk. After that, the commission ran smoothly.

This is the clearest argument for the test garment we can offer. A measurement can look like a perfectly ordinary number on a form. It is only when we see how that number behaves on a real body that we know whether it was the right number.
