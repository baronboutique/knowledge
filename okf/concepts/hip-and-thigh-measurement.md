# Hip and Thigh Measurement

Size charts often instruct customers to measure the hips a fixed distance below the waist, commonly seven or eight inches. We do not, and the reason explains most of what follows: bodies are not built to the same proportions, and the fullest point of the hip occurs at different distances below the natural waist from one person to the next. A rule that works for one customer puts the tape in the wrong place on another.

## Where the tape goes

The tape passes around the fullest part of the hips and seat, and stays level all the way around.

The most reliable approach is to stand naturally in front of a mirror and identify where the hip and seat are fullest, then place the tape there. The seat is part of this measurement. We are not measuring the hip bones at the sides, which is a common misreading of the word "hips" and produces a number several inches short.

Stand naturally with the feet in a comfortable position. We prefer feet close together or in a normal relaxed stance rather than a wide one, which changes the shape being measured. The goal is the body's normal standing circumference, not the largest circumference a stance can create.

## The things that ruin it

Measuring over clothing is the most common problem, and the hip is where it does the most damage. Jeans, thick pants, a phone in a pocket, a wallet, anything between the tape and the body adds to the number. Take everything out of the pockets and measure over underwear or very light, close-fitting clothing.

Hands are the second offender. Do not hold the tape in place with your hands underneath it; your hands belong outside the tape. A hand under the tape adds its own thickness in exactly the place you are trying to measure.

The tape must stay level. If it slopes up at the front or back it is no longer measuring the true circumference, and again the resulting number looks entirely plausible.

The rule is short: remove anything that is not part of the body, stand naturally, keep the tape level, and let the tape follow the body rather than compress it.

## The thigh

For the thigh we want the fullest part of the upper thigh, measured standing. We do not recommend measuring while seated, because sitting changes both the shape and the circumference. The tape goes around the thigh without compressing the flesh.

For trousers we normally want the larger thigh when there is a noticeable difference between the legs. A garment has to accommodate the larger measurement, and smaller differences can be handled through the pattern.

For a fitted skirt the thigh may or may not be needed, depending on the design and how closely the skirt is meant to fit through that area. A straight or relaxed skirt does not require the same thigh information as a closely fitted skirt or a pair of tailored trousers. The garment decides whether the measurement matters, which is why our sets differ by garment rather than collecting everything every time.

## What a wrong hip number does

A hip error affects the entire lower silhouette.

Too small, and a skirt or dress pulls horizontally across the seat and hips. A trouser becomes tight through the seat, throws diagonal drag lines, pulls the pockets open, and rides upward because there is not enough room where the body needs it.

Too large, and the garment simply looks loose and loses the shape it was designed to hold.

A fitted trouser is the least forgiving garment we make when the hip is wrong, because the seat, crotch, thigh, and leg all interact: a small error at the hip changes how the whole trouser hangs. A fitted skirt runs a close second, particularly where there is very little ease through the hip. This is why we never judge the hip in isolation but read it against the waist, thigh, height, and the design.

## When the proportions look unusual

When the waist-to-hip relationship looks unusual for the stated height, we do not assume the measurement is wrong. People are shaped differently, and an unusual ratio is very often completely correct.

What we do is read it against everything else we hold. If the hip is significantly different from what the other measurements imply, we may ask the customer to verify it. Photographs help us understand frame and proportion. So does the archive: more than 26 years and thousands of completed garments give us a reference for what tends to go together.

That archive is not used to push a customer toward a standard shape. It helps us recognize when a number deserves a second look. Two customers can share a height and a waist measurement and still need very different hip measurements and pattern shapes, and we do not try to make them fit the same block.

The body determines the pattern. The numbers only help us describe the body.
